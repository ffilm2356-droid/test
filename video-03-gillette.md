### CHƯƠNG 1 — King Gillette's idea (1:05–3:00)

> In 1895, a traveling salesman named King Camp Gillette was shaving with a straight razor that needed constant sharpening. He had an idea: a blade so thin and cheap you'd throw it away when it went dull.
>
> Engineers told him it couldn't be done. Steel that thin wouldn't hold an edge. It took an MIT-trained engineer, William Nickerson, years to figure out how to harden and sharpen sheet steel. Gillette's safety razor with disposable blades was patented in 1904.
>
> Business schools love to say Gillette gave away the razor and made money on the blades. The real history is more interesting. While the patents lasted, Gillette actually charged a high price for the razor too. The giveaways came later, after the patents expired in the early 1920s and competitors could copy the handle.
>
> What Gillette really discovered was this: once a handle is in your bathroom, you'll keep buying the blades that fit it. For a very long time.

**Hình ảnh:** ảnh tư liệu King Gillette (public domain) + lower-third "King C. Gillette, 1904"; bản vẽ bằng sáng chế; dây chuyền ép tôn thép cổ.

### CHƯƠNG 2 — The machine (3:00–5:00)

> For the next hundred years, Gillette did one thing over and over. Add a blade, raise the price. Two blades in 1971. Three blades in 1998 with the Mach3 — a product the company said cost about seven hundred fifty million dollars to develop. Five blades in 2006.
>
> In 2005, Procter & Gamble bought Gillette for about fifty-seven billion dollars. At its peak, Gillette held around seventy percent of the U.S. razor market.
>
> The other big name, Schick, now owned by Edgewell, had most of what was left. Two companies, one aisle, and prices that only went one way.

**Hình ảnh:** đồ họa "1 → 2 → 3 → 5 BLADES" kèm giá tăng; thanh đỏ "≈70% U.S. MARKET"; org-chart P&G (nền đen, chữ trắng).

### CHƯƠNG 3 — "Our blades are f***ing great" (5:00–7:15)

> In March 2012, a man named Michael Dubin uploaded a video to YouTube. Ninety seconds. Shot in a warehouse. Budget: about forty-five hundred dollars.
>
> The pitch was simple: a dollar a month, mailed to your door. The blades came from a manufacturer in South Korea, Dorco.
>
> The servers crashed within an hour. Twelve thousand orders in two days. Dollar Shave Club had found the crack in Gillette's wall. The wall wasn't the blade. It was the shelf. If you never walk into the drugstore, the locked plastic case doesn't matter.
>
> In 2013 a second startup, Harry's, did the same thing — and then did something smarter. In 2014 it bought its own blade factory in Eisfeld, Germany, a nearly hundred-year-old plant, for about one hundred million dollars. Now it didn't just sell blades. It made them.

**Hình ảnh:** nhà kho, thùng carton, máy dán nhãn; flycam thị trấn nhỏ Đức; macro lưỡi dao chạy trên băng chuyền.

### CHƯƠNG 4 — A billion dollars, and an eight-billion-dollar hole (7:15–9:15)

> In 2016, Unilever bought Dollar Shave Club for one billion dollars in cash.
>
> Gillette had to respond. In 2017, Procter & Gamble cut Gillette prices by up to twenty percent. It launched its own shave club.
>
> By 2018, Gillette's U.S. share was down to roughly forty-seven percent. Dollar Shave Club alone held over eight percent.
>
> In 2019, Procter & Gamble took an eight-billion-dollar write-down on the Gillette business. Eight billion dollars of value — gone — partly because people stopped buying blades the way they used to.

**Hình ảnh:** thanh đỏ 70% co lại 47%; tờ báo cáo tài chính overlay "$8B WRITEDOWN"; người đàn ông để râu (xu hướng không cạo).

### CHƯƠNG 5 — The government steps in (9:15–11:30)

> Then, in May 2019, Edgewell — the owner of Schick, the number two razor company — agreed to buy Harry's for about one point three seven billion dollars.
>
> On paper, it looked like a normal deal. A big brand buying a hot startup.
>
> The Federal Trade Commission didn't see it that way. In February 2020, it sued to block the merger. Its argument: Harry's was a "uniquely disruptive competitor". It had forced Gillette and Schick to cut prices and offer more choices. If Schick swallowed Harry's, that pressure would disappear.
>
> Less than a week later, Edgewell walked away.
>
> Think about what that means. The U.S. government looked at the razor aisle and decided one startup with a German factory was the main reason prices had come down.

**Hình ảnh:** văn bản khiếu nại FTC (bản công khai) cạnh dao cạo; timeline lưới trắng, marker đỏ "FEB 2020"; tay đóng tập hồ sơ.

### KẾT — So what does a blade cost? (11:30–12:30)

> So what is a blade really worth? A few slivers of steel, a strip of plastic, a lubricating strip. Industry estimates put the manufacturing cost of a cartridge far below its shelf price. The rest is a hundred years of marketing, patents, and shelf space.
>
> What brought the price down wasn't a better blade. It was someone who found a way around the aisle — and a government that decided that way had to stay open.
>
> If you want to see how one company quietly ended up owning half the speaker aisle, watch this next.
