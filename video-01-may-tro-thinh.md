### CHƯƠNG 1 — The price nobody questioned (1:05–3:00)

> Roughly thirty million American adults could benefit from a hearing aid. Fewer than one in three older adults who need one has ever worn one. The number one reason they give isn't vanity. It's price.
>
> A typical pair in the prescription market sells for four to six thousand dollars. Insurance usually doesn't cover it. Traditional Medicare doesn't cover it at all.
>
> Here's the part most people never see. That price isn't just the device. For decades the industry sold hearing aids "bundled": the hearing test, the fitting, years of adjustments, all folded into one number. You couldn't buy the box without buying the service. And you couldn't buy the box at all without going through a licensed dispenser.
>
> That rule didn't come from the companies. It came from regulators in the 1970s, worried about people buying useless devices from mail-order ads. It was meant to protect patients. Over time, it also did something else. It made sure every single sale passed through a gate. And whoever controlled the gate controlled the price.

**Hình ảnh:** người lớn tuổi đưa tay lên tai trong bữa tối gia đình; hóa đơn y tế; đồ họa "BUNDLED PRICE" tách thành 3 khối (Device / Test / Fitting); ảnh tư liệu quảng cáo báo giấy thập niên 70.

### CHƯƠNG 2 — The Big Five (3:00–5:00)

> If you lined up every hearing aid sold on the planet, more than ninety percent would come from five companies. Sonova, from Switzerland. Demant, from Denmark. WS Audiology, from Denmark and Singapore. GN, also Danish. And Starkey, from Minnesota.
>
> Sonova alone holds more than thirty percent of the world market.
>
> To most shoppers, the brand names on clinic walls look like competition. Phonak. Unitron. Oticon. Bernafon. Signia. Widex. ReSound. But many of those names roll up to the same handful of parents.
>
> And the gate? Several of the Big Five also own retail chains. Demant owns HearingLife. Sonova owns AudioNova. The company that builds the device can also own the clinic that recommends it.
>
> Even the warehouse-club hearing aids that undercut the clinics — widely reported to be made by one of the Big Five. The cheap option and the expensive option, different badge, same family.

**Hình ảnh:** đồ họa org-chart kiểu video A (nền đen, chữ trắng, 5 cây công ty mẹ → logo con); flycam trụ sở Thụy Sĩ bên hồ; thanh đỏ "SONOVA 30%+".

### CHƯƠNG 3 — The rule change (5:00–6:45)

> In 2017, two senators who agree on almost nothing — Elizabeth Warren and Chuck Grassley — co-sponsored a bill to let adults buy hearing aids over the counter.
>
> The industry pushed back hard. Safety concerns. Self-diagnosis. Damage from devices that were too loud.
>
> It took five more years. In August 2022, the FDA issued its final rule. On October 17th, 2022, over-the-counter hearing aids became legal for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss. No exam. No prescription. No dispenser.
>
> Within months, more than a hundred OTC devices were registered with the FDA. And a lot of them came from the same place your phone charger comes from.

**Hình ảnh:** ảnh hai thượng nghị sĩ (ảnh báo chí có giấy phép) hoặc minh họa tòa Capitol; timeline lưới trắng, marker kim cương đỏ "OCT 17, 2022"; kệ Walmart/CVS với hộp OTC.

### CHƯƠNG 4 — Shenzhen enters the room (6:45–8:30)

> Strip a hearing aid down and there are only a few parts that matter. A microphone. A tiny speaker called a receiver. A battery. And a digital signal processor — a chip that amplifies the frequencies you've lost.
>
> Twenty years ago that chip was cutting-edge. Today, the same kind of audio processing runs inside fifteen-dollar wireless earbuds. Southern China makes most of the world's earbuds. So when the U.S. opened the door, Shenzhen factories didn't need to invent anything. They only needed to tune what they were already building.
>
> On Amazon today, entry-level OTC pairs sell from under a hundred dollars to a few hundred. They are not as good as a professionally fitted premium device. For severe hearing loss, they're not even legal. But for the millions with mild loss who were buying nothing at all, "good enough at a hundred dollars" beats "perfect at four thousand."

**Hình ảnh:** teardown macro từng linh kiện (ánh sáng gắt, nền tối như video A); dây chuyền SMT; trang sản phẩm Amazon (chụp màn hình thật, che tên shop nếu cần); split-screen $99 vs $4,000.

### CHƯƠNG 5 — The earbud that became a medical device (8:30–9:45)

> Then, in September 2024, the FDA authorized something no one in the industry saw coming that fast: a hearing aid feature running on Apple's AirPods Pro 2. A software update. Millions of people already had the hardware in their pockets.
>
> Now, the Big Five weren't caught sleeping. GN sells OTC devices under Jabra. Sonova bought Sennheiser's consumer audio business. Starkey launched its own OTC line. They are doing what Harman did with speakers — making sure that whichever aisle you walk down, the money ends up in the same few places.

**Hình ảnh:** tai nghe trắng trên nền xám xoay chậm; màn hình iPhone bài kiểm tra thính lực (mockup trung tính, không logo); đồ họa: 3 logo tiêu dùng nối mũi tên về công ty mẹ.

### KẾT — So what does the four-thousand-dollar pair cost? (9:45–11:00)

> So what are you really paying for? Part of it is real: an audiologist's time, a proper fitting, follow-ups that genuinely help people with serious hearing loss.
>
> But part of it was the gate. For forty years, the gate meant five companies never had to compete with a factory in Shenzhen or a phone in your pocket. In 2022, the gate opened.
>
> The prices on the clinic wall haven't collapsed. Not yet. But for the first time in two generations, they have to explain themselves.
>
> If you want to see the same story play out with eyeglasses, it's on screen now.
