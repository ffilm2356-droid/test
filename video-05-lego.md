### CHƯƠNG 1 — The carpenter's son (1:05–3:00)

> In 1932, a Danish carpenter named Ole Kirk Christiansen started making wooden toys in the village of Billund. He called the company LEGO, from the Danish "leg godt" — "play well."
>
> In 1958 his son Godtfred patented the version of the brick we know today. The secret wasn't the studs on top. It was the tubes underneath. They gripped the studs just tightly enough — "clutch power" — so a model held together but still came apart.
>
> That patent made LEGO. And patents end. By 1978, the last of the core brick patents had expired in most of the world.

**Hình ảnh:** ảnh tư liệu Billund (đen trắng, lower-third "Billund, Denmark, 1932"); đồ chơi gỗ; mặt cắt viên gạch hoạt hình 3D cho thấy ống bên dưới.

### CHƯƠNG 2 — LEGO vs everyone (3:00–5:00)

> LEGO spent the next thirty years trying to protect the brick another way: trademark law. If the shape itself was a trademark, it could last forever.
>
> In Canada, it fought Mega Bloks all the way to the Supreme Court. In 2005, it lost.
>
> In Europe, the fight went to the EU's top court. In 2010, the court ruled the shape of the brick was functional — and a functional shape can't be trademarked.
>
> The message from the courts was clear. The brick belongs to everyone. What LEGO can protect is its name, its logo, its minifigure designs, its box art and the specific sets it designs.

**Hình ảnh:** tòa nhà Tòa án Tối cao Canada & Tòa án Công lý châu Âu (ảnh có giấy phép); timeline lưới trắng: 1978 ● 2005 ● 2010; búa tòa.

### CHƯƠNG 3 — Lepin (5:00–7:30)

> Which brings us to Lepin.
>
> Starting around 2015, a factory in Shenzhen began producing building sets. Not just compatible bricks. Copies of LEGO's own sets — the same models, the same instructions, the same box art, sold online at a fraction of the price. Some even launched before the official LEGO version reached stores.
>
> That was the line. Not the brick. The design work, the instructions, the packaging — that's copyright. And copying it at that scale is a crime in China, too.
>
> In April 2019, Shanghai police raided the operation. They seized hundreds of thousands of finished sets and the molds used to make them.
>
> In September 2020, a Shanghai court sentenced the ringleader, a man identified only by his surname, Li, to six years in prison and a ninety-million-yuan fine. Eight others got three to four and a half years. The appeals court upheld the verdicts.

**Hình ảnh:** nhà kho chất đầy thùng hàng (minh họa, không phải hiện trường thật); khuôn ép kim loại macro; tay cảnh sát niêm phong thùng (dựng lại, ghi chú "illustration"); văn bản bản án đặt cạnh viên gạch.

### CHƯƠNG 4 — The legal challenger (7:30–10:00)

> If the story ended there, it would be a simple one. Copycat gets caught. Brand wins.
>
> But while Lepin was being shut down, other Chinese companies were learning the lesson. Make the brick — that's legal. Make your own designs — that's legal. Buy the license for characters people love.
>
> One of them is Blokees, owned by Bloks Group. It licensed characters like Ultraman and Transformers and built small, cheap, collectible figures you snap together in minutes.
>
> According to industry analysts at Frost & Sullivan, Blokees is now the third-largest construction toy brand in the world, and the largest in China, with around thirty percent of China's assembly toy market. It listed on the Hong Kong stock exchange in early 2025. In the first half of 2026, its overseas revenue grew more than two hundred percent.

**Hình ảnh:** figure lắp ráp nhỏ màu sắc trên bàn trắng (không logo nhân vật thật); thanh đỏ "No.3 WORLDWIDE"; bảng điện tử sàn chứng khoán Hồng Kông.

### CHƯƠNG 5 — Why LEGO isn't panicking (yet) (10:00–11:30)

> LEGO is still the giant. Revenue of more than seventy-four billion Danish kroner in 2024 — over ten billion dollars. It even built a factory in China itself, in Jiaxing, back in 2016.
>
> But look at what changed. For decades, LEGO's threat was a cheaper version of the same thing. It won that fight in court, again and again.
>
> The new threat isn't a copy. It's a different idea — cheaper, faster, built around characters LEGO doesn't own — made by companies that read the same court rulings LEGO did, and stayed exactly on the legal side of the line.

### KẾT (11:30–12:15)

> So where is the line between a brick and a crime? The brick is free. It has been since 1978. What you can't take is somebody else's idea.
>
> Lepin took the idea and went to prison. Blokees brought its own and went public.
>
> If you want to see a company that sued a rival for making something that looked too similar, this one's next.
