### CHƯƠNG 1 — Beijing, August 2008 (1:10–3:00)

> On August 1st, 2008, one week before the Beijing Olympics, China opened its first true high-speed line. Beijing to Tianjin. About a hundred and twenty kilometers. Trains running at three hundred fifty kilometers an hour.
>
> Three years later, in June 2011, the Beijing–Shanghai line opened. More than thirteen hundred kilometers — roughly the distance from New York to Chicago. Built in about three years.
>
> And they didn't stop. By the end of 2024, the network had passed forty-eight thousand kilometers. The plan is sixty thousand by 2030.

**Hình ảnh:** ảnh tư liệu Olympic Bắc Kinh 2008 (có giấy phép); tàu CRH lướt qua đồng lúa; bản đồ timelapse 2008 → 2024 (đường đỏ lan dần).

### CHƯƠNG 2 — California, November 2008 (3:00–5:00)

> Three months after Beijing–Tianjin opened, California voters approved Proposition 1A. Nearly ten billion dollars in bonds to start a high-speed line from Los Angeles to San Francisco. The promise: two hours and forty minutes, city to city. Running by 2020.
>
> 2020 came. No trains.
>
> Today, the state's own estimates put the full cost somewhere between eighty-nine and one hundred twenty-eight billion dollars. The first segment — in the Central Valley, between Merced and Bakersfield, not between the big cities — is now targeted for the early 2030s.
>
> In 2025, the federal government pulled about four billion dollars in grants from the project.

**Hình ảnh:** phiếu bầu cử minh họa "PROP 1A"; flycam công trường Central Valley; bảng timeline lưới trắng với các mốc lùi dần (2020 → 2029 → 2033), marker kim cương đỏ.

### CHƯƠNG 3 — Where the money went (5:00–7:30)

> So why does the same idea cost so much more in one place?
>
> Start with cost per kilometer. A 2019 World Bank study found China built high-speed rail for roughly seventeen to twenty-one million dollars per kilometer. That's about two-thirds of what it typically costs elsewhere in the world. California's line is on track to cost several times more per kilometer.
>
> Part of it is land. In California, the project has had to buy well over a thousand individual parcels from private owners — farms, homes, businesses — each one a negotiation, sometimes a lawsuit.
>
> Part of it is law. Environmental reviews, which exist for good reasons, can take years and can be challenged in court segment by segment.
>
> Part of it is skill. China built thousands of kilometers year after year, with the same crews, the same designs, the same factories pouring the same standard concrete beams. California is building its first.

**Hình ảnh:** biểu đồ thanh "$ per km": China vs World vs California (thanh California chạy ra khỏi khung); bản đồ địa chính các mảnh đất nhỏ; xe lao dầm khổng lồ đặt dầm bê tông lên trụ (footage Trung Quốc).

### CHƯƠNG 4 — The bill China doesn't show you (7:30–10:00)

> But before we hand China the trophy, look at the other side of the ledger.
>
> China's state railway operator carries a mountain of debt — trillions of yuan. Many high-speed lines, especially in poorer, less crowded provinces, don't carry enough passengers to cover their costs. Analysts have long reported that only a handful of the busiest routes, like Beijing–Shanghai, actually turn a profit.
>
> And in 2011, just weeks after the Beijing–Shanghai line opened, a high-speed train crash near Wenzhou killed forty people and forced the country to slow its trains and rethink its safety culture.
>
> So China proved one thing beyond doubt: it can build fast, cheap and at enormous scale. Whether every one of those forty-eight thousand kilometers was worth building is a question it is still answering.

**Hình ảnh:** ga tỉnh nhỏ vắng người; thanh đỏ "DEBT" chồng cao; ảnh báo chí lưu trữ (có giấy phép) hoặc khung hình tối trừu tượng cho đoạn tai nạn — không dùng hình ảnh nạn nhân.
